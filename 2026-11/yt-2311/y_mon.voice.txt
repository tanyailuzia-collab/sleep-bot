The first hour of the night has a special structure. A child's sleep is made of cycles of forty to sixty minutes, and in the first hour she goes through the first, and sometimes the second, transition between them. At every transition the brain partly surfaces and checks that everything is the same as when she fell asleep. If something is off, she doesn't dive back in. She wakes up crying. Moms tell me the hardest part of these evenings is the feeling that the night hasn't even started, and there's already nothing left in the tank. Reason one. Overtiredness that looks like joy. A child who has been awake too long before the night often doesn't look tired at all. She looks wired. She laughs, she runs, she doesn't yawn. That isn't energy. It's a backup battery: stress hormones the body switches on once tiredness has crossed the line. They help her fall asleep fast, but at the very first cycle transition they push her out of sleep. If you decide she's just not tired yet and put her down later, the wake-ups only multiply. So the first thing I check is whether the evening awake time is longer than the daytime ones. Reason two. Built-up tiredness. Sometimes the schedule has already settled, the days are calm, and the first hour of the night is still broken. That's tiredness built up over previous weeks. It takes two to three weeks to fade, and nights are the last thing to improve. Moms often decide at this point that the plan isn't working and change everything. In fact, you just need to give time a chance to do its job, and not change the plan on day three. Reason three. How she falls asleep. Whatever a child falls asleep with, she looks for each time she surfaces between cycles. If she falls asleep in your arms, in motion, or at the breast, at the first transition she looks for the same thing. And the first hour holds exactly one or two of those transitions. It isn't hunger and it isn't a whim. It's simply how she knows how to fall asleep. Change it after the schedule is in place, in small steps, and without tears. Reason four. The transfer during light sleep. After about seven months, the first ten to fifteen minutes of sleep are shallow. A baby put down at minute three gets woken by everything: a cool sheet, a creak, a change in smell. If you transfer her, wait until her breathing is deep and even and her arms go floppy. If the days are already calm and the nights aren't, give them two more weeks. A few tips moms often hear that make the first hour worse. Putting her down later so she's really tired: overtiredness only grows. Feeding at every evening wake-up: she learns she has to eat at every cycle transition. Turning on the light and carrying her to another room: the brain gets the signal that the day goes on. Instead, help minimally and quietly: darkness, the same white noise, a hand on her back, a calm voice. And do the main work during the day, in the awake times. Moms are often surprised that the best help for the first hour of the night happens at two in the afternoon. But that's how the arithmetic of the day works: whatever was under-played or over-played during the day comes out in the first cycle of the night.
